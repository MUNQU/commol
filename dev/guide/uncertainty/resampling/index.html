    Resampling: Bootstraps and the Jackknife¶

    Back to Uncertainty

    These methods give the uncertainty of a calibration with no noise model and no prior. They fit the model again to reweighted or resampled data, starting from the fit, and read the spread of the new fits. Use them when you can say how the data were collected but not how the observations scatter around the model. Each runs through Calibrator.uncertainty, in parallel in Rust (crates/commol-inference/src/resampling/).

    Quick path¶

    import numpy as np

from commol import (
    Aggregate,
    CalibrationParameter,
    CalibrationProblem,
    Calibrator,
    ModelBuilder,
    NelderMeadConfig,
    ObservedDataPoint,
    Simulation,
    scores,
)
from commol.resampling import (
    BayesianBootstrap,
    BlockJackknife,
    ResidualBootstrap,
    WildBootstrap,
)
from commol.observations import NoNoise, ResidualNoise
from commol.uncertainty import Predictive


def ab_model(k: float | None):
    return (
        ModelBuilder(name="AB")
        .add_bin(id="A", name="A")
        .add_bin(id="B", name="B")
        .add_parameter(id="k", value=k)
        .add_transition(id="t_ab", source=["A"], target=["B"], rate="k")
        .build(
            Aggregate(bin_amounts={"A": 1000, "B": 0}),
            engine="DifferenceEquations",
        )
    )


# Data with a multiplicative noise of 5 %: fit steps 1 to 60, hold out the rest.
truth = Simulation(ab_model(0.05)).run(num_steps=80)
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
observations = [
    ObservedDataPoint(
        at=step,
        output="B",
        value=truth["B"][step] * float(np.exp(rng.normal(0.0, 0.05))),
    )
    for step in range(1, 81)
]
fitted = [point for point in observations if point.at <= 60]
held_out = [point for point in observations if point.at > 60]

problem = CalibrationProblem(
    observed_data=fitted,
    parameters=[
        CalibrationParameter(id="k", parameter_type="parameter", min_bound=0.0, max_bound=0.5)
    ],
    loss_function="sse",
    optimization_config=NelderMeadConfig(max_iterations=500),
    seed=42,
)
calibrator = Calibrator(Simulation(ab_model(None)), problem)

result = calibrator.uncertainty(BayesianBootstrap(replicates=100, block_length=1))
lower, upper = result.sample.interval(0.9)["k"]
print(round(lower, 4), round(upper, 4))  # 0.0483 0.0512

    result.fit is the fit, result.sample holds one draw per replicate that fitted, result.failed lists the replicates that failed, and result.diagnostics gives the block length.

    Which method to use¶

    Method Changes in each replicate Assumes Answers
    BayesianBootstrap The weight of each block of observations The blocks are exchangeable How far the parameters move when the data are reweighted
    ResidualBootstrap The observed values: fitted values plus blocks of residuals drawn with replacement The model is right; the residuals of each block are exchangeable across positions How far the parameters move under new noise like the fit's
    WildBootstrap The observed values: each residual times a random sign or Mammen multiplier, one per block The model is right; the noise has mean zero at each position The same, when the size of the noise changes along the series
    BlockJackknife One block left out (weight 0) per fit The blocks are exchangeable A standard error and a bias, and which block moves each parameter most

    None of them needs a noise model. The Bayesian bootstrap never changes the observed values, so it also works when the model is wrong; the residual and wild bootstraps make new data from the model and its residuals. With observation models, ParametricBootstrap(replicates) draws new data from the models at the fit; it draws no blocks, so its diagnostics.block_length is None.

    Blocks and their length¶

    A block holds every observation, of every series, whose position falls in one window of block_length consecutive distinct positions (BlockScheme, resampling/blocks.rs). The dependence of the data across series and along the positions stays inside blocks, so the methods hold for noise that is correlated in time or between series.

    block_length is a whole number of at least 1, with no default: it is your decision, about your data. 1 states that the noise of different positions is independent. A longer block states that the noise depends on its neighbours no further than the block holds. Every method that reads blocks, here and on the other pages, takes it so.

    commol does not choose it. The best length changes with the method and with the number of blocks, and one number has no single answer for several series of different dependence.

    The length counts positions¶

    • Of all the series together. A block holds consecutive distinct positions of the data, whichever series has an observation there. With a weekly series beside a daily one, the positions are days: a length of 7 holds one value of the weekly series, and 4 of its values need 28.
    • Also where no observation carries information. A series that the model holds at exactly 0 until a process starts says nothing about the parameters while it is observed at 0: those scores are 0 for the count models (Poisson, negative binomial, binomial), for a normal or Student t model whose sd, scale and df are numbers, and for a loss without normalize_observations. A model with a fitted sd, scale or df reads them, and so does the normalization of a series: there they change the result at any position. Where the scores are 0, at a position that another series also observes they change nothing in the Sandwich or in InformationMatchingEta. At a position that only such observations hold, it still counts: it takes a place in a window, so it shifts the windows after it, and a window of such positions alone is one more block.

    Choose the length¶

    1. Read the dependence of the residuals of the fit with residual_dependence: for each series its autocorrelations, the integrated autocorrelation time τ with its band, and cut_lag, the largest lag in the sum of τ. They count steps of the grid of that series, so a lag of 3 of the weekly series is 21 days. A fit absorbs dependence and a short series hides it (The bias of τ): a small τ does not show that the noise is independent.
    2. Use what you know of the process: a weekly pattern of the reports, an event that moves several positions, the time that a disturbance lasts. A cycle at a long lag does not reach τ.
    3. Compare the result at several lengths, with the number of blocks each leaves, for the method and the parameters you report.

    The same model with a noise that depends on its neighbours, an AR(1) of 0.7:

    from commol.dependence import Refitted, residual_dependence

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
shock = np.zeros(61)
for t in range(1, 61):
    shock[t] = 0.7 * shock[t - 1] + rng.normal(0.0, 0.05)
dependent = [
    ObservedDataPoint(at=t, output="B", value=truth["B"][t] * float(np.exp(shock[t])))
    for t in range(1, 61)
]
dependent_calibrator = Calibrator(
    Simulation(ab_model(None)), problem.model_copy(update={"observed_data": dependent})
)
fit = dependent_calibrator.run()

(series,) = residual_dependence(
    Predictive(dependent_calibrator, dependent_calibrator.point_sample(fit), positions=[1]),
    dependent,
    NoNoise(),
    max_lag=10,
    randomizations=1,
    reference=Refitted(replicates=99),
    confidence_level=0.9,
).series
print([round(r, 2) for r in series.correlations[:4]])  # [0.6, 0.28, 0.16, 0.03]
print(series.p_value, series.cut_lag)  # 0.01 3
print(round(series.tau, 2), [round(b, 2) for b in series.tau_band])  # 3.09 [0.52, 2.05]

for length in (1, 3, 6, 10, 15):
    compared = dependent_calibrator.uncertainty(
        BayesianBootstrap(replicates=300, block_length=length)
    )
    low, high = compared.sample.interval(0.9)["k"]
    blocks = -(-len(dependent) // length)
    print(length, blocks, round(high - low, 4))
# 1 60 0.003
# 3 20 0.0049
# 6 10 0.0058
# 10 6 0.0049
# 15 4 0.003

    The residuals depend on their neighbours: the test rejects independence, τ is above its band, and the autocorrelations fall to about 0 at lag 4. Blocks of one position give the narrowest interval, since they ignore that dependence. From 3 to 10 positions the interval keeps about one width, and blocks of 15 leave only 4, whose spread says little: the interval narrows again although the data did not change.

    A range of lengths with one width is not a range with the coverage of the level. Over 2000 data sets of this example, the 90 % interval of k covers its value in this share of them (300 replicates, or 4000 draws of the sandwich):

    block_length 1 3 6 10 15
    Blocks 60 20 10 6 4
    BayesianBootstrap 46 % 61 % 66 % 64 % 62 %
    Sandwich 48 % 67 % 78 % 84 % 87 %

    No length brings the bootstrap near 90 %: 60 values with this dependence leave few independent blocks, and its spread does not allow for that. The sandwich does, with a Student t of one degree of freedom less than its blocks, and its interval is also wider with few of them. Comparing lengths shows how far the choice moves the result, not which length is right: report the length with the result, say when the result changes with it, and, when the series is short, that the interval can cover less than its level (Short series).

    A method also sets how few blocks it takes:

    Method Blocks it needs
    BayesianBootstrap, WildBootstrap, ResidualBootstrap None: they take one block, with no note
    BootstrapCoverageEta None, but with one block its replicates are equal and tell it nothing
    BlockJackknife, JackknifePlus, InformationMatchingEta At least 2
    Sandwich More than the parameters
    EnbPI Each left out by at least one replicate

    Information matching gives the degrees of freedom that its blocks read, and warns when they are few; the sandwich reads a Student t with one degree of freedom less than its blocks.

    With a length of all the positions, one block, the bootstraps draw nothing: each replicate of BayesianBootstrap, and of ResidualBootstrap with "moving", has the data of the fit, so its interval has width 0; that of WildBootstrap is about 0 in this example, and "stationary" gives its narrowest. No note says so: read the number of blocks.

    A series with two observations at one position is refused by ResidualBootstrap, which draws one residual per position, whatever the length; the other block methods accept it (One position, two observations).

    Bayesian bootstrap¶

    BayesianBootstrap(replicates, block_length, starts=1) draws, for each replicate, one Dirichlet(1, …, 1) weight per block, scaled to mean 1, and multiplies the weight of every observation of the block by it. With a loss it is the loss-likelihood bootstrap of Lyddon, Holmes and Walker (2019); with block_length=1, the Bayesian bootstrap of Rubin (1981). Its variance matches the sandwich variance of least squares (tests/uncertainty/test_resampling.py::TestBayesianBootstrap).

    Residual block bootstrap¶

    ResidualBootstrap(replicates, block_length, residual_scale, scheme, starts=1) takes the residuals of the fit, joins blocks of them drawn with replacement, and fits the new data.

    Setting Values
    residual_scale "additive": r = y − ŷ, new data ŷ + r*. "multiplicative": r = log(y / ŷ), new data ŷ · exp(r*); it needs positive observed and fitted values, and keeps the new data positive
    scheme "moving": blocks of block_length consecutive positions (Künsch 1989). "stationary": blocks of geometric length with mean block_length, wrapped around the end (Politis and Romano 1994)

    Use the multiplicative scale for counts or amounts that span orders of magnitude, where the noise grows with the value. On the additive scale a new value can fall below zero: the method keeps it, and counts it in diagnostics.negative_values. Many of them point to the multiplicative scale.

    residual = calibrator.uncertainty(
    ResidualBootstrap(
        replicates=100,
        block_length=1,
        residual_scale="multiplicative",
        scheme="stationary",
    )
)
print(residual.diagnostics.negative_values)  # 0

    When a series has no observation at a drawn position, it takes the residual of the nearest position where it has one.

    Wild block bootstrap¶

    WildBootstrap(replicates, block_length, multipliers, starts=1) multiplies the additive residuals of each block by one draw of multipliers and fits ŷ + r · v. Every residual stays at its own position, so the method holds when the size of the noise changes along the series, where the residual bootstrap mixes small and large residuals.

    multipliers Distribution
    "rademacher" ±1 with probability 1/2
    "mammen" Two values with mean 0, variance 1 and third moment 1 (Mammen 1993); keeps the skewness of the residuals 
    wild = calibrator.uncertainty(
    WildBootstrap(replicates=100, block_length=5, multipliers="mammen")
)
print(len(wild.sample))  # 100

    Block jackknife¶

    BlockJackknife(block_length, starts=1) fits once per block, with that block left out, and returns a JackknifeResult. With B blocks it needs B fits, usually far fewer than a bootstrap.

    Field Meaning
    estimate The bias-corrected estimate of each parameter
    bias (B − 1)(θ̄ − θ̂), with θ̄ the mean of the fits with one block left out
    standard_error √((B − 1)/B · Σ (θ̂₍₋ᵦ₎ − θ̄)²)
    blocks The first and last position of each block that fitted
    pseudo_values B θ̂ − (B − 1) θ̂₍₋ᵦ₎ per block
    influence How far leaving each block out moves each parameter, in standard errors
    failed Each block whose fit failed, "Block <index> failed: <reason>", with blocks numbered from 0 in order of position; also logged as a warning
    not_converged Fits kept although their optimizer did not converge
    diagnostics The block length, as for a bootstrap

    The pseudo-values are not draws: their percentiles are not an interval, so the result has no ParameterSample. A block with a large influence holds data the fit depends on: check it.

    jackknife = calibrator.uncertainty(BlockJackknife(block_length=10))
print(jackknife.blocks[:2])  # [(1.0, 10.0), (11.0, 20.0)]
print(len(jackknife.influence["k"]))  # 6

    The result¶

    A bootstrap returns a BootstrapResult(fit, sample, failed, not_converged, diagnostics). The sample has equal weights, and the group of each draw is its replicate.

    Field Meaning
    failed Each replicate that failed, "Replicate <index> failed: <reason>"; it is not in the sample, and it is logged as a warning
    not_converged Replicates kept although their optimizer did not converge
    diagnostics.block_length The block length the method ran with; None for ParametricBootstrap
    diagnostics.negative_values New observed values below zero, over every replicate

    Every result of a method that fits several times lists the fits that failed in failed and counts the fits kept without convergence in not_converged, in the same way: a bootstrap, the jackknife, a profile with a bootstrap threshold and the conformal methods that refit. When too few fits succeed, the method raises a RuntimeError that says how many of how many fitted, how many it needs, and the first reasons.

    result.interval_error(confidence_level) gives the Monte Carlo error of each bound of the central interval: an order-statistic interval that holds the bound of infinitely many replicates with about 95 % probability. When it is wide against the interval, run more replicates.

    error = result.interval_error(0.9)["k"]
print(error.lower[0] <= lower <= error.lower[1])  # True

    How replicates run¶

    Each replicate starts from the fit. With starts > 1 it also starts from the other starts of the optimizer, and keeps the end of lowest loss, so a second basin of the loss shows up instead of adding optimizer noise to the data's uncertainty. Replicate b draws its data and its starts from streams of the problem's seed, one for the data and one for the fits, at index b: two runs with one seed give one result. A replicate changes only the observed values or the weights (CalibrationProblem::with_data); with normalize_observations its normalization comes from its own values, as the fit's does.

    Since every replicate starts from the fit, the fit must run and be feasible. When no point of the bounds meets the constraints, the fit ends on the penalty (How a violation counts) and can still say it converged, and each replicate would stay there: an interval of width 0. So a bootstrap and the jackknife raise a ValueError for a fit that:

    • violates a constraint, which the message names by its id,
    • has a predicted mean outside the means its observation model allows,
    • or has a loss or a likelihood that is not a finite number.

    ResidualNoise does not refit, so it reads the residuals of such a fit.

    Prediction intervals¶

    The draws of a bootstrap give a band of the model. ResidualNoise adds moving blocks of residuals of the fit to every draw's prediction of each observation, which gives a prediction interval with no noise model. It serves the series the fit has residuals of, at any position, and rejects an observation of any other series. It and ResidualBootstrap draw one residual per position of a series, so they refuse a series with two observations at one position, in the fit or in the observations they serve (One position, two observations).

    predictive = Predictive(calibrator, result.sample, positions=list(range(81)))
noise = ResidualNoise(
    fit=result.fit,
    block_length=result.diagnostics.block_length,
    residual_scale="multiplicative",
)

print(scores.coverage(predictive, held_out, NoNoise(), confidence_level=0.9).mean)  # 0.05
print(scores.coverage(predictive, held_out, noise, confidence_level=0.9).mean)  # 0.85

    The band of the model is narrow and covers 1 of the 20 held-out observations; the prediction interval covers 17, near its level.

    Short series¶

    A block bootstrap reads the dependence of the noise from few blocks. On a short series its variance is too small by a term of order 1 / block_length, and its interval covers less than its level. In the coverage study of tests/uncertainty/test_resampling_coverage.py, a 90 % interval of a series with AR(1) noise (φ = 0.5) covers about 0.8 with 200 positions and about 0.89 with 400. With few positions, prefer block_length=1 only when the noise is independent, and report that the interval may be narrow.

    Checklist¶

    • [ ] You chose block_length from the dependence of the residuals and the scale of the process, read the result at several lengths, and report the length.
    • [ ] You chose the residual scale for the data: multiplicative for values that span orders of magnitude.
    • [ ] failed is empty, or you know why replicates failed.
    • [ ] not_converged is 0, or you raised max_iterations.
    • [ ] interval_error is small against the interval.
    • [ ] You report a band of the model as a band of the model, and use ResidualNoise for a prediction interval.
